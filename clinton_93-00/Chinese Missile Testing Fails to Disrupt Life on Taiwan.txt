
The day after three unarmed Chinese missiles fell near Taiwan's shores, unsubtle weapons in a campaign of intimidation, leaders here made the expected calls for calm and vowed to repel any attacks.
But among the people of Taiwan, long accustomed to living in the shadow of a hostile giant, only sporadic signs of anxiety punctuated a day that differed little from any other. 
Traffic jams clotted Taipei's streets, with motor scooters weaving madly between honking taxis, lumbering buses and streams of delivery trucks. 
And the campaign for the presidency, at the forefront of many people's concerns, continued its furious pace.
Typical was the response of Wang Chi-san, a dock worker at this cargo and fishing port on the northern tip of the island. 
As a crane swung a rope sling piled with crates of brain-numbing kaoliang alcohol onto a truck at dockside here, he broke into a laugh. 
"Eeverybody's a little worried," he said, "but really, there's no reason to be afraid. 
No reason at all."
Only 12 hours before, an unarmed Chinese M-9 ballistic missile had slammed into the sea barely three hours' sail north of this port, marking the onset of a new Chinese campaign to intimidate this island of 21 million people as it edges toward its first presidential election in two weeks.
In the predawn hours, two other missiles crashed into the South China Sea west of one of Asia's major container ports at Kaohsiung. 
It was the closest that Chinese missiles, armed or unarmed, have come to this island in 57 years.
Throughout the day, China kept up a rattle of bellicose statements, with Foreign Minister Qian Qichen warning today that "a real disaster" would befall Taiwan if it persisted in its quest for independence.
As dawn's glow nibbled at the darkness, President Lee Teng-hui was out in the street markets of Taipei campaigning and chatting with shoppers. 
Huge crowds cheered his arrival, but he refrained from commenting on the missile tests.
A spokesman for the Foreign Ministry, Michael Chen, said that Mr. Lee preferred to let other Government organs speak on the issue.
Kao Koo-liang, the vice chairman of the Mainland Affairs Council, accused China of trying to intimidate Taiwan's people as they prepare to vote in the island's first presidential elections. 
And despite mounting threats from Beijing about Taiwan's efforts to elevate its posture internationally, Mr. Kao said Taiwan was fully prepared to defeat a military assault by China.
"We will respond to this unreasonable provocation with reason and self-restraint," he said, "but we are thoroughly prepared to deal with an armed invasion from the mainland."
"As long as we can consolidate our psychological defenses, unite together and remain steadfastly determined in our pursuit of freedom and democracy, Beijing will fail in its attempt at armed intimidation."
As if to emphasize this confidence, the Taipei stock market soared more than 54 points, raising the index 1.14 percent to 4,805.09, a sharp reversal from the panicky selloffs earlier this week.
At the same time, however, local banks continued to report runs on American dollars, with many banks running out as the day wore on. 
The Bank of America announced that it would fly in a plane loaded with United States currency on Monday to cope with the demand.
Taiwan's television channels hurtled back and forth between soap operas, celebrity cooking contests and blanket coverage of the missile tests. 
Boats chugged out into the mist with miserable-looking correspondents peering into the mist. 
Reporters hovered around radar screens in air traffic control terminals, the screens glowing with red boxes marking the air exclusion zone declared by China. 
And passengers and pilots were buttonholed to see how they felt about the five-minute delay of their flights.
Yet, even as China seemed bent on cowing Taiwan into some sort of submission, submission leading to reunification with the mainland, the missile tests appeared to be having an opposite effect.
"Generally speaking, people are maintaining calm," said Andrew Nien-tsu Yang, the secretary general of the Chinese Council of Advanced Policy Studies, a research body in Taipei. 
"But there's a lot of discussion about the missiles."
He continued: "Some people are very frustrated about China firing the missiles. 
It's making people distasteful about China's behavior."
Here in Keelung, a gritty industrial town, there were few signs of apprehension, however. 
But the local lighthouse minder, Su Teh-tsun, found his five minutes of glory as local television stations barraged him with questions about his night.
"I never heard anything like it," Mr. Su said on local television, comparing the crack of the missile into the sea with "an exploding tire" that jolted him awake shortly after midnight.
Here, in the closest spot in Taiwan to the target zone, there was more bemusement at the invasion of journalists poking microphones in faces than concern over the missiles.
"It's our impression that conditions are normal," said Han Te-an, the director of the Keelung Harbor Bureau. 
"One ship from Okinawa was delayed. 
But nothing else."
"I think all this is an overreaction," he said, waving a hand dismissively through the humid air. 
"I don't think this will affect Taiwan."
Just in case, though, the county executive, Yu Ching, took a small boat out toward the target zone to show his constituents that at least he would not be intimidated. 
On dockside, Thomas Wang, a harbor policeman who was keeping an eye on the unloading of the crates of kaoliang alcohol, said that activity in the harbor was the same as always. 
But, he growled, "we want to fight them."
International reaction to China's missile firings was overwhelmingly negative. 
In Tokyo, Prime Minister Ryutaro Hashimoto said that he regretted China's actions and described Beijing's policy orientation toward Taipei as taking an "unfortunate direction."
A Japanese coast guard vessel was moved south to check on shipping routes, according to the Reuters news service.
And though life here went on much as it does every day, a young woman dashed through the rain into the harbor master's office, pausing only to seethe, "I hate them. 
I hate them."
