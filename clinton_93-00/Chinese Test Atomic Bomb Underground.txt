
China continued its testing of a new generation of ballistic missile warheads by exploding a nuclear device beneath its far western desert at Lop Nor today.
In Washington, the White House said it "deeply regrets" the Chinese test, and the State Department called on China to abide by a moratorium on nuclear testing, which has been honored by the other nuclear powers since 1992. 
"We continue to urge China to consider the changed circumstances of global security affecting the role of nuclear weapons," the department spokesman, Michael McCurry, said.
Today's test, like an earlier one in June and another one a year ago, is part of a series that China has undertaken to verify the reliability of warheads designed during the 1980's, experts said.
One Chinese scientist who worked inside China's ballistic missile program said the new warheads were intended to be smaller than China's older ones, yet more powerful in their explosive yield.
"Right now, China is testing its miniaturization program," the scientist said in a recent interview. 
"If China obeys the international request to stop testing nuclear weapons, then it means China would have to cancel its ballistic missile program without developing its second-generation ballistic missiles."
China's nuclear strategy is based on assuring it the ability to retaliate after any nuclear first strike against it by another country.
The detonation was detected by the Australian Seismological Center in Canberra at 11:25 A.M. today Chinese time (11:25 P.M. Thursday E.D.T.). 
Center officials estimated its explosive strength at 40 to 150 kilotons, the equivalent of 40,000 to 150,000 tons of TNT.
The Chinese Foreign Ministry later confirmed the test, saying Beijing would put an end to its nuclear tests as soon as negotiations on a comprehensive nuclear test ban treaty were completed in 1996.
"Among all the nuclear states, China has conducted the least nuclear tests," the ministry's statement said. 
"It has always exercised great restraint on the question of nuclear tests and will continue to do so in the future."
It was China's 41st nuclear test since 1964, compared with 44 tests by Britain, 210 by France and more than 1,000 by the United States.
In 1993 the Clinton Administration invested a substantial amount of energy trying to persuade the Chinese leadership to abandon its testing program. 
After China's test last October, President Clinton instructed Energy Secretary Hazel O'Leary to begin reviewing options to resume American testing at the Nevada test range.
When this threat drew no response from the Chinese, the White House conceded that nothing it could do in the form of pressure could dissuade Beijing, and the effort was abandoned.
But Foreign Minister Qian Qichen, during a visit to Washington this week, agreed to work with the United States to ban the production of weapons-grade uranium and plutonium to prevent proliferation and, eventually, to halt the production of nuclear material for use in new weapons by the countries that have nuclear weapons.
