
Officials here initially responded with surprising calm to China's latest denunciation of Britain's plans for political change and its rejection of talks on the sensitive matter.
The colony's governor, Mr. Patten, said he remained eager to open discussions on his proposed changes.
A number of Hong Kong politicians called news conferences to declare that they still hoped there could be talks with Beijing, and many said they supported the British stand that Hong Kong officials must be part of any talks on the city's future.
The responses seemed resigned, almost rote. 
As a stock broker explained: "We had heard all these attacks before, and they could have been worse. 
There was nothing new."
Though Mr. Lu said that Mr. Patten would be "condemned" by history, the British governor, asked his reaction, said, "I don't want to respond to that sort of remark."
He added, "We'll start talks tomorrow if Chinese officials would like."
Mr. Patten called on the Chinese to explain why they had insisted that local officials be excluded from the negotiating table, when for the last 10 years they had been part of British teams negotiating with Beijing.
Mr. Lu had been careful to insist that China would abide by the Joint Declaration on transferring control of Hong Kong and other touchstone agreements. 
To that, Mr. Patten responded, "If Chinese officials are saying they will operate entirely within the terms of the Joint Declaration, then I can say that we have something in common."
