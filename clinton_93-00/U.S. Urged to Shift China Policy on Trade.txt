
Robert E. Rubin, director of the National Economic Council, says he would like the Clinton Administration to ultimately adopt a China policy that ended the link between trade and human rights.
At a breakfast with journalists on Friday, Mr. Rubin repeated the Administration's view that China needed to make more progress on human rights to win annual renewal of its preferred trading status with the United States. 
But pointing to the size of China's economy, he added that ending the link between human rights and trade "is a very good objective to shoot for."
Mr. Rubin said it would have to be done in a way that assured people about human rights, but conceded that he did not know how to maintain leverage on human rights if they were separated from Washington's trade policy with Beijing.
"I haven't heard anybody, even the people advocating delinking, suggest exactly how this might be done," he said. 
"But I suspect it is not undoable."
In last year's debate within the Administration on whether to extend China's most-favored-nation trade status, Mr. Rubin was one of the strongest advocates of renewal. 
Shedding light on his reasoning on Friday he said: "There's no question that absent some unexpected event, China will be the largest economy in the world not too far into the next century. 
It is imperative that we have an enormous economic relationship with" it.
