
Few New York City spectacles radiate as much anxiety as the annual surge of eighth graders outside Stuyvesant High School, the educational Valhalla on the Hudson, for a one-shot, grueling entrance exam that many believe will determine the rest of their lives. 
The most recent test one icy winter day -- with scores released just last month -- was, as usual, full of held-back tears, stomachaches and dreams.
More than 1,000 students, from recent Chinese immigrants to girls from the exclusive Brearley School, huddled behind police barricades this past December as teachers with bullhorns told parents to "kiss your loved ones goodbye."
Then they retreated to wait and worry.
Everyone knew that Stuyvesant, although bitterly criticized by minorities for a test they say benefits students from districts with white majorities, remains a nonnegotiable meritocracy. 
Connections, money or a dazzling interview will not help a student get in. 
The sole criterion for admission is still the two-and-a-half-hour entrance exam that Adam Robinson, the co-founder of the Princeton Review test-preparation service, considers more difficult than the Scholastic Assessment Test taken in 11th grade. 
The average college-educated adult, he said, could not be admitted to Stuyvesant without preparation.
What follows is the story of three talented eighth-graders, from different backgrounds, who took the Stuyvesant test in December for admission as freshmen this fall. 
Their stories show how Stuyvesant, a free public school in lower Manhattan, has been seen for nearly a century as an Ivy League dream-ticket for the poor, the rich and especially the immigrant. 
In some years, Stuyvesant sends more students to Harvard than any other public or private school in the nation. 
(Last year 21 of 700 seniors were admitted to Harvard, 20 to Yale, 47 to Columbia and 103 to Cornell.)
Jing Wang, 12, lives on the Lower East Side. 
She is shy, talkative once you get to know her, and academically relentless. 
She started studying for the Stuyvesant test on her own in sixth grade. 
She came to the United States from China when she was 4, and excels at Dr. Sun Yat-sen Intermediate School 131 in Chinatown, which in 1996 was shut out of Stuyvesant -- no one from the school passed the test. 
Jing's father was a radiologist in China but is now unemployed.
Heather McLeod, 14, lives in the Crown Heights section of Brooklyn. 
She is sunny, enthusiastic and determined, and has wanted to go to Stuyvesant since she was in fifth grade. 
She has a 94 grade-point average at Mark Twain Intermediate School for the Gifted and Talented, a magnet school in Coney Island that is one of the best public junior highs in the city. 
It is also one of Stuyvesant's biggest feeder schools. 
Heather's parents are separated, and her father lives in Jamaica. 
Her mother, a medical clerk, makes $27,000 a year.
Adam Bloch, 13, lives on the Upper West Side. 
He is, already, something of a wry intellectual, who can discuss Henryk Sienkiewicz, the Polish novelist, as easily as "The Simpsons," one of his favorite shows. 
He is a top student at the private Bank Street School on West 112th Street. 
He also applied for ninth grade to three private schools: Horace Mann, Fieldston and Dalton. 
His father owns a graphic design firm. 
His mother, who has stayed home, will soon start graduate school in social work.
For Jing, Heather, Adam and anyone else, getting into Stuyvesant is harder than ever. 
This year, 11,397 eighth graders competed for 850 spots, meaning fewer than one in 13 got in. 
In 1991, it was more than one in 12, when 6,675 eighth graders competed for 580 spots. 
Jinx Cozzi-Perullo, Stuyvesant's principal, says the applicant increase is largely a result of the luxurious, $150 million building that opened in 1992. 
(Statistically, getting into Stuyvesant is harder than admission to Harvard, which this month will accept 2,073 students from 16,818 applicants, or one in 8.)
Jing, Heather and Adam were recommended by their schools and interviewed before they received their test results. 
Their teachers said each had an excellent chance of getting in. 
But only two did.
 
Jing Wang
Excellence At English, Too
"I want to go to Harvard," said Jing Wang. 
"But then, a lot of people want to go to Harvard."
Jing was talking after school in her fourth-floor walk-up on East Second Street, in a cramped two-bedroom apartment with a big television set and stacks of Chinese videotapes. 
She was apprehensive at first, then pleased to be asked questions about herself and her views, and generally tolerant of her 5-year-old sister, who hovered around wanting to be interviewed, too.
Her teachers say Jing is one of the brightest students in a school that is 80 percent Asian, and where even those born in America have difficulty with English because they don't speak it at home, in Chinatown or with their friends at school. 
Jing's English is idiomatic, and rapid. 
"I have an idea I'm at the top of the class," she said matter-of-factly. 
She wants to be a pediatrician, or maybe a lawyer.
Jing's father, An Yin Wang, came to the United States in 1987 from northwest China, studied radiology at Mount Sinai Hospital, and worked as a sonographer at North General and then at a Chinese-run clinic on the Lower East Side. 
He was laid off from that job last fall, he said, because the manager hired cheaper Chinese help. 
His wife had joined him in New York in 1988, but Jing remained in China until her parents were able to send for her in January 1990. 
She cried with fear when she first saw them at the airport in New York. 
"They were like total strangers," she said. 
"I wanted to go back to China with my grandmother."
Jing does two to four hours of homework a night -- only then does she watch TV -- but last fall was also in a self-created total immersion course as she counted down to the Stuyvesant test. 
On Saturdays she took a test preparation course at I. S. 131, offered free to the best 30 students based on previous test scores. 
On Sundays she took a private preparation class given by the Kaplan testing service, at $700, which her father paid for before he was laid off. 
She also studied S.A.T. preparation books that she checked out of the library. 
To relax, she read horror fiction for young adults.
The Stuyvesant test is officially called the "Examination for the Specialized Science High Schools" -- Stuyvesant, the Bronx High School of Science and Brooklyn Technical High School. 
The same test is given for admission to all three, and students simply list their first, second and third choice. 
Since a majority of students list Stuyvesant first -- 11,397 out of 18,524 eighth graders who took the most recent test -- the cutoff for admission to Stuyvesant is higher. 
This year students had to score 559 or better for Stuyvesant's admission, or get about 85 of the 100 questions right.
The test is divided into English and math sections, just like the S.A.T., and is prepared and graded by American Guidance Service, an educational publishing company outside Minneapolis-St. 
Paul. To make the test more fair to immigrants whose English lags, sections on sentence completion and word synonyms, antonyms and analogies have been eliminated. 
The English part of the Stuyvesant test emphasizes "logical reasoning," which educators say makes the test more difficult.
"It's an indication of a more sophisticated learner," said Carolyn West, an I. S. 131 teacher who helped prepare Jing for the test.
A sample question for the "logical reasoning" section goes like this:
Five students are in line according to height, from tallest to shortest. 
Liz is taller than Mickey but shorter than Fred. 
Mickey is shorter than Noah but taller than Judy. 
Noah is shorter than Fred. 
In which position is Noah?
A. first B. second C. third D. fourth E. cannot be determined from the information given.
To get the answer, Ms. West said, a student has to be able to think enough to draw a diagram for help, from tallest to shortest. 
But after plotting out the information in each sentence, all a student can learn of Noah is that he goes somewhere between Mickey and Fred. 
A student still doesn't know how Noah compares with Liz. 
The answer is E.
Jing headed off to Stuyvesant at 7 A.M. on Dec. 
6, nervous. 
A week later, she talked to a reporter, whom her father later escorted to the neighborhood subway stop.
"I cannot make big money," he said quietly as he walked, describing the difficulties of a China-trained doctor in the United States. 
"But for my daughter, the future is important. 
After many years, she can be a specialist."
 
Heather McLeod
'I Want to Go To Harvard'
Heather McLeod was not nervous about the Stuyvesant test until her friend Justin told her that his life depended on passing it. 
"He said, 'If I don't make it to Stuyvesant, I can't get into a good college, and I can't make a lot of money and I'll live on the street,' " she recalled.
Heather laughed, but she had her heart set on Stuyvesant, too. 
"I want to go to Harvard, I really do," she said. 
"So many people from Harvard get someplace."
She and her mother, Sylvia, were talking the night before the test in their living room in the Jackie Robinson Apartments, a complex built over Ebbets Field. 
Lots of families like the McLeods live there, but Heather feels different. 
"The kids say, 'Come out and play,' and I'll say, 'I have a book report due.'
And they'll say, 'You're a nerd.'
"
Heather's parents -- her father worked in a boiler room, her mother has a high school education -- came from Jamaica 28 years ago. 
"Our children are going to do well in this country," Mrs. McLeod said firmly. 
She is pleased so far. 
Heather's two older brothers went to parochial schools, then Georgetown and Fordham. 
One is now a mid-level manager at the Federal Aviation Administration, the other a manager at a Marriott Hotel. 
Neither passed the Stuyvesant test, but Mrs. McLeod has always had bigger hopes for Heather. 
"She thought farther ahead than her brothers did," she said.
Heather gives her mother all the credit. 
"She read to me every night when I was little," she said later in the evening, her eyes growing moist, when her mother had left the room. 
"Golden Books, Cinderella, children's Bible stories. 
I don't know how she did it, but she made me want to excel."
Heather started studying for the Stuyvesant test last summer and by fall was spending her school lunch hours in a test prep course offered free by the Mark Twain School. 
"They drilled it into you," she said. 
She was well aware of how few black students pass the test, but said she was not going to think about that.
Blacks account for 4.1 percent of Stuyvesant's student body, Latinos 3.7 percent. 
(The rest of the student body is 47.7 percent Asian and 45.5 percent white.) The numbers outrage community advocacy groups like Acorn, which in 1997 released a report, "Secret Apartheid II," showing that private and parochial schools plus three public school districts provided more than half the students at Stuyvesant and Bronx Science.
The report found that the five districts that sent the most students to the schools were predominately white, and the five that sent the fewest were predominately black and Latino. 
The report also said schools in those districts offered few of the advanced courses that help in passing the test.
Acorn recommended suspending the test altogether, an idea rejected by Schools Chancellor Rudy Crew. 
But the Board of Education now has an 18-month prep course, the Math/Science Institute, housed at Stuyvesant, for 400 mostly minority students. 
Students are selected based on standardized test scores and principals' recommendations, and then by lottery. 
The institute is financed by the Board of Education at a cost of $1.2 million a year. 
Of the first Math/Science group, which started after sixth grade in the summer of 1995, 304 took the test and 45 got in -- 10 of them black and 11 Latino. 
"It has to start somewhere," said Bruce Ravage, the institute's director. 
The institute has just added three new sites in other boroughs, for 700 more spots.
 
Adam Bloch
Outsmarted By His Cleverness
Adam Bloch vividly remembers the last math question on his Stuyvesant test: i is a 28-consecutive number set. 
What is the maximum number of multiples of seven that can be in i?
"Four times seven is 28 -- you immediately think that," Adam said. 
He decided the answer was obviously four.
And yet, it seemed a little too obvious. 
His test-preparation teachers had told him to think, and this answer didn't seem worthy of the Stuyvesant test. 
Adam decided it must be a trick.
He skipped up to seven through 35, calculating that since seven plus 28 is 35, it was another 28-consecutive number set. 
And that got him the multiples of 7, 14, 21, 28 and 35. 
The answer, he decided, was 5.
After the test he saw his mistake. 
Seven through 35 is in fact a 29-consecutive number set. 
The right answer, all along, was 4. 
"Looks like it's a double-trick," Adam said. 
"They try to trick you into thinking when you shouldn't think."
Adam talked easily at the dining table in his high-rise apartment, decorated with his parents' collection of Judaica, as his mother, Ofra, wandered in and out. 
She once worked in an art gallery in Tel Aviv, and came to the United States in 1980. 
Adam's father came from Poland in 1972.
Adam seemed amused to be interviewed, and offered many opinions about books, movies and history. 
Talking to him was a lot like talking to an adult, if the average adult had his discipline.
He said that when he arrives home after school, "I've always had this tradition: I say hi to my mother, and I rush into my room."
There he always does his homework -- before a snack, before phone calls.
Adam loved studying Greek mythology and Minoan Crete at Bank Street last fall -- he read the Iliad and the Odyssey -- and the Middle Ages in the sixth grade. 
He said he is currently reading "American Sphinx: The Character of Thomas Jefferson" by Joseph J. Ellis. "It's not a book I would dive into and read for several hours a day," he admitted. 
"But it's interesting."
For 10 weeks last fall, Adam took a private test preparation course, GRF, costing about $500, and was one of 26 students at Bank Street -- which goes only through eighth grade -- who applied to Stuyvesant. 
Adam's older brother goes to Stuyvesant. 
When he was taking the test, Mrs. Bloch said, money was a concern.
Adam said he thought private school tuition was $17,500 a year.
"No, it's 18 and a half," his mother told him gently. 
Then she sighed. 
"It's a stretch, of course it is."
 
The Results
'I Didn't Cry Or Anything'
The results came in the first week of March, delivered to the three by their schools: Adam and Jing got in, Heather did not.
"I didn't cry or anything," Heather said. 
"But I was disappointed."
She scored 483, meaning she got about 70 of 100 questions right. 
Jing got a 584, or about 88 right. 
Adam got 625 and about 93 right, ranking 118 out of the more than 18,000 who took the test.
Jing, who was speechless when she got the news -- "I was like really, really happy" -- will go to Stuyvesant. 
Adam, who thinks his test-preparation course may have added 50 points to his score, was one of six students from Bank Street who made the Stuyvesant cutoff. 
Adam also got into his three private schools. 
After thinking it over, he decided that Stuyvesant, with its 3,000 students, was too big. 
He'll go to Dalton.
Heather did qualify for Brooklyn Tech, but next year will go to Bishop Ford Catholic Central High School in the Bay Ridge section of Brooklyn, as her brothers did. 
"She'll do fine no matter where she goes," said Gary Goldstein, Heather's principal. 
"She'll be an exemplary student."
