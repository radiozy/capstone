
China conducted an underground nuclear test today, announcing later that it would stage just one more test of its nuclear arsenal by September before joining an international ban on such explosions.
The test -- and the promise to stop -- came just two days after China softened its stance and agreed to include nonmilitary explosions in a nuclear test ban treaty now being negotiated in Geneva.
The Foreign Ministry said in a statement put out by the state-run New China News Agency after the blast:
"China will continue to work with other countries for the conclusion, within this year, of a fair, reasonable and verifiable treaty with universal adherence and unlimited duration."
Australian scientists who monitored the test said China set off the explosion at 10:56 A.M. in the Lop Nor test range in the northwestern province of Xinjiang.
The New China News Agency confirmed that China had conducted the test but provided no details. 
It said China would conduct one final test before the fall.
The blast had an estimated yield of 20 to 80 kilotons, making it a middle-range explosion, said the Australian Seismological Center and the Australian Geological Survey Organization. 
The bomb that the United States dropped on Hiroshima in 1945 was about 12.5 kilotons.
According to the news agency, the Foreign Ministry said China had only a limited nuclear arsenal, for the purpose of self-defense, and that it "does not constitute a threat to any country."
The ministry said the final tests -- aimed at developing lighter, more powerful weapons, according to Western experts -- were the minimum necessary to safeguard China's interests in the face of other countries' nuclear arsenals and the "threat of a nuclear war."
The test was the first recorded since Aug. 
17 and at least the 44th explosion detected at Lop Nor since testing began there in 1964, the Australian organizations said.
China is the only declared nuclear power still conducting tests.
At test-ban talks in Geneva, China announced on Thursday that it would drop its long-held demand for the right to continue tests for what it calls "peaceful purposes" like huge construction projects or power generation.
The announcement encouraged negotiators hoping to reach an agreement on the test-ban treaty by June 28.
China has said it will abide by the nuclear test ban treaty once it takes effect but it has also sought to delay that date -- apparently to allow time to conclude its tests.
China had widely signaled today's test, with Japanese news organizations reporting on Thursday that Japanese officials had been informed that a test was coming.
The international proposal calls for an indefinite ban on all tests.
