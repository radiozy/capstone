
Officially, power in China is concentrated in the hands of a small number of men, all career Communist politicians who were alive when the People's Republic was declared on Oct. 
1, 1949.
But today a new class of leaders is emerging that will form China's public face in the decades to come. 
They are men and women in their 30's and 40's whose aims and experiences exemplify the growing diversity of China after two decades of market-oriented change.
While ultimate power remains tightly linked to Communist Party politics, many members of the new generation exert influence in a wide variety of other fields, like business, art and science. 
And they tend to differ from the ruling generation, more flexible and cosmopolitan in their ideas.
"They automatically accept reform; they know it must happen," said Li Qiang, a sociologist at People's University in Beijing. 
"They have often been abroad and welcome the cultures of other countries."
Born toward the end of the tumultuous Maoist era, many younger adults did not internalize the fear and caution so essential to survival then.
"Most older Chinese have the habit of listening to the radio in the morning, because they did so every day in the 1960's to learn what Mao wanted them to do," Professor Li said. 
"The younger generation is more independent; it doesn't look for instructions."
Some emerging leaders, to be sure, join the Communist Party and pursue Government careers. 
In a country where the party still has the final say in every field, most people who are out to make a mark are careful to cultivate official ties.
The individuals profiled below represent an arbitrary selection, in Beijing, from the multitudes of rising young stars. 
Together, they provide a taste of the China being born.
