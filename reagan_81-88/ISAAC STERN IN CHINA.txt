
THEY call it ''From Mao to Mozart - Isaac Stern in China,'' and the title of this delightful film that opens today for a five-day run at the Symphony Space gets the general idea across.
Our American violin virtuoso, a remarkable, cheerful and cherubiclooking man, visited that calisthenics-smitten nation, marveled at the extraordinary nimbleness of mind and body, shrugged his shoulders and observed, ''On the other hand, they can't play Mozart.''
Mr. Stern by no means deprecates his hosts in this account of his visit to China in June 1979. 
Indeed, he marvels at the talent developing there, and his love for the musicians and students he met overflows in his singularly expressive and good-humored way. 
This is a film that will teach you something about China but more about Mr. Stern. For those who have not met the man behind the violin before, it offers insights into his deeply humanistic approach to music.
He took something to China that is not prevalent there: the realization that the music must come from the heart and soul rather than from the instruments alone. 
The director of a conservatory of music in Shanghai - an older man who suffered during the Cultural Revolution - explains how young Chinese set great store by technical skill. 
They want hard pieces, loud and fast, to demonstrate how well they can use instruments. 
And in so doing, they miss the point of the music itself.
Mr. Stern and David Golub, the pianist who often appears with him, are seen in performance; negotiating for a piano to replace the unusable one they found in Shanghai; getting a tourist's-eye view of China.
The film, which has been nominated for an Academy Award in the best-documentary category, produced and directed by Murray Lerner, with Allan Miller as artistic supervisor. 
It is a beautifully made documentary that does not presume to tell all about China or Mr. Stern but does give us a most enjoyable record of a productive visit.
