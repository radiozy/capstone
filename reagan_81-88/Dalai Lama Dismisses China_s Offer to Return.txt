
The Dalai Lama, the exiled spiritual leader of Tibetan Buddhism, today dismissed China's offer to allow him to return to Tibet if he abandoned his campaign for independence. 
He said there was nothing new in the offer, which was made Monday, and indicated that he would not accept the restrictions.
''I have had that invitation from the Chinese Government since 1979,'' he said in London.
The Dalai Lama's visit to Britain is his first overseas trip since anti-Chinese rioting broke out in the Tibetan capital, Lhasa, last September - there were new riots in March. 
The 52-year-old spiritual leader, who fled to India in 1959 after Chinese troops put down a nationalist rebellion, has long been an outspoken advocate of human rights and independence for the people of Tibet.
In brushing aside China's public offer to return to Tibet, the Dalai Lama stressed, instead, the value of his own campaign from exile for independence for the foreseeable future. 
He expressed a willingness to meet with the Chinese authorities, if there were genuine signs of improved treatment of Tibetans by the Chinese.
The Dalai Lama emphasized the need to solve the Tibetan problem through what he called ''the middle way - some kind of compromise.''
The middle way, according to the pacifist spiritual leader, lies between harsh rule by China and a fierce struggle for independence, the course advocated by some young Tibetan militants.
The Dalai Lama generally struck a conciliatory tone toward the Chinese authorities, saying he would be willing to meet with them in Beijing to discuss the Tibetan issue.
