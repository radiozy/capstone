
Senate and House committees voted today to approve President Reagan's nuclear cooperation agreement with China, but only after imposing restrictions designed to prevent the spread of nuclear material around the world.
A key Administration official indicated Mr. Reagan would accept the measure reluctantly.
James Devine, Deputy Secretary of State for Nuclear Energy, said that while the Administration would have preferred no restrictions, ''We have looked at this and concluded it will not undercut the implementation of the agreement.''
Senator Alan Cranston, Democrat of California, a key critic of the original pact, praised the legislation, saying it reinforced safeguards while avoiding an ''unpleasant confrontation'' with the White House and China.
The bill was cleared by voice vote by the House Foreign Affairs Committee, while the Senate Foreign Relations Committee approved it by an 11-to-3 margin. 
It was not immediately clear how soon the measure would reach the floor of the Senate or House for a vote.
After negotiating the agreement with Peking, the Reagan Administration submitted it to Congress, where critics said it lacked safeguards.
Senator Cranston, in a speech on the Senate floor, charged that China was a ''confirmed proliferator'' that had engaged in either nuclear negotiations or trade with five ''nuclear outlaw'' nations believed to be trying to develop atomic weapons. 
Senator Cranston named the five as Brazil, Argentina, Pakistan, South Africa and Iran.
Under the legislation, the President may provide nuclear material to China only after certifying to Congress that he has ''additional information'' from Peking that it is not helping other countries acquire atomic weapons.
