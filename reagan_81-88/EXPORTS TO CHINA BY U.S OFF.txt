
A Chinese boycott of American agricultural products last year cut American exports to China by more than a third, according to preliminary Government figures released today.
Huang Wenjun, a spokesman for the Ministry of Foreign Economics and Trade, also said at a news conference that China's total foreign trade reached $40.14 billion in 1983, up 2.2 percent from 1982, despite what he called a sluggishness in demand.
China's exports last year were $22 billion and its imports $18.14 billion, leaving a trade surplus of $3.86 billion. 
This is less than the $4.7 billion surplus in 1982.

Mr. Huang said China's exports to the United States had remained at the 1982 level, while its imports from the United States dropped 38.3 percent. 
He gave no specific statistics, but there have been estimates that total Chinese-American trade dropped to less than $4.3 billion last year, from nearly $5.2 billion in 1982.
 
Curbs on Farm Products
The sharp drop in China's imports came after China refused to purchase United States grain and other agricultural commodities in retaliation for American quotas placed on the purchase of Chinese textiles. 
A new textile agreement was reached last August, but China failed to meet its commitment to buy a minimum of 6 million tons of grain annually under a long-term purchasing agreement extending through 1984.
Last month, the Chinese Embassy in Washington told the United States Government that China would make up its obligation by buying the remaining 2 million tons of grain for delivery in 1984. 
It bought only 4 million tons last year.
The United States Embassy here said Friday that China imported $1.8 billion worth of American goods in the first 11 months of 1983, while exporting $2.1 billion in Chinese products. 
If this trend continues, it would give China its first trade surplus with the United States since relations were normalized between the two countries.
 
Textile Exports to U.S. 
Climb
Despite Washington's imposition of controls on Chinese textiles, sales of Chinese clothing and accessories to the American market climbed 25 percent in the first 11 months of last year, to $713.9 million.
Chinese-American trade has declined from its high point of $5.5 billion in 1981. 
The Washington-based National Council for United States- China Trade predicted earlier this month that commerce between the two countries could bounce back to a record $5.5 billion to $6 billion this year, because of the Reagan Administration's move to loosen restrictions on the sale of advanced technology to China.
Mr. Huang told reporters today that Hong Kong remained the leading market for China's exports. 
Hong Kong bought $5.3 billion worth of Chinese goods last year, a 6.6 percent increase over 1982.
