
When Richard S. Ondrik flew from Peking to this city on April 18, he was as prepared as an American can be for the trade opportunities unfolding in China.
Fifteen hours later, choking on acrid smoke, the Indiana-born energy expert was trapped in a nightmare. 
A fire had broken out in Harbin's Swan Hotel. 
On the 11th floor, an American working with Mr. Ondrik on an oil equipment deal, Alan Eng, was dead. 
The bodies of nine other men who had chosen to jump rather than endure the intense smoke and flame lay on the ground. 
Seven others were seriously injured. 
Chinese investigators quickly zeroed in on Mr. Ondrik as the cause of the fire, accusing him of having fallen asleep with a lighted cigarette. 
After days and nights of interrogation without a lawyer, and a 10-week delay while the matter was bounced around the bureaucracy in Peking, Mr. Ondrik was formally arrested and taken to a detention center where a naked light bulb burned throughout the night. 
After a few days he was formally charged with the ''unintentional crime'' of setting fire to the hotel.

The ensuing trial ended in July after four days of hearings, and a verdict is expected by Aug. 
15. At the worst, Mr. Ondrik could end up with a seven-year jail term and a bill for compensation to the hotel of $87,600.
Even if the court is lenient, as the chief judge suggested in his closing remarks, the American could spend years paying off damages for which he is uninsured. 
Worse, he may never again be able to function effectively as a businessman in China, a career for which he studied Mandarin and otherwise prepared himself for 10 years.
In some ways he is caught in a web of circumstances that are peculiar to China. 
Long after the case is closed what happened will stand as a warning to Westerners.
Unlike United States courts, Chinese ones do not require a plea of guilty or not guilty, and Mr. Ondrik has staked out a position somewhere in between. 
His account to the investigators, repeated in court, was that he had no recollection of lying down on his bed with a cigarette, but that after the shock of the fire he could not be sure.
 
An American Attorney
In affidavits presented to the court by his attorney, Richard C. Goodwin Jr. of Washington, five former roommates attested that they had never seen the American smoking on a bed.
In the end Mr. Ondrik's case rests less on whether he was responsible for the fire than on the more basic question of whether he should be on trial at all. 
Although the article of the Chinese criminal code under which the charge was laid bears some similarity to the United States crimes of criminal negligence and involuntary manslaughter, Mr. Goodwin, for one, says that he knows of no case in which those charges have been laid in an American court against somebody accidentally setting a fire with a cigarette. 
Was the Hotel Safe?
Mr. Ondrik's dinner was sponsored by the Harbin Oil Refinery, to whom the American's Hong Kong-based company, Energy Projects Southeast Asia Ltd., was hoping to sell a compressor. 
At the end of a day that saw the American fly 750 miles and conduct hours of talks, he consumed, by his own testimony, eight to 10 glasses of maotai, four glasses of wine and several beers.
Everything seems to have conspired against the victims. 
Prosecution evidence showed that rooms in the 16-month-old hotel had been designed for smoke and fire detectors, and were wired for them, but the devices themselves remained in storage. 
Plastic wallpaper and hollow plywood doors speeded the fire and there were no emergency exit signs. 
The only fire extinguisher did not work, and there was no alarm. 
The floor attendant, on a 24-hour shift, was off taking a bath and a meal on the eighth floor. 
Some of the testimony implied that the fire exit was locked.
Worse yet, the fire department was not answering its telephone. 
Later, taxi drivers at the hotel said that when the the firetrucks finally arrived, 37 minutes late, they had been fetched by a hotel car.
 
North Koreans Died in Fire
After Mr. Ondik's arrest on June 26, Mr. Goodwin had argued at the Foreign Ministry and elsewhere that a trial would be harmful to trade and other ties with the United States. 
But the Chinese were confronted with a more compelling diplomatic consideration, the fact that five of those who died and four of the injured were members of a North Korean Government delegation. 
In the lawyer's view, that ultimately made a trial inevitable.
Barely a week separated Mr. Ondrik's indictment from the opening of the trial, and by the time Mr. Goodwin reached Harbin there were only four days left. 
As a foreigner he was barred from appearing in court or seeing his client in detention, and had to work instead through a Chinese attorney he had engaged in Peking.
The careful collection of physical evidence and statements supporting the state's case marked an improvement on the past, when criminal trials, when they were held at all, were arbitrary in the extreme. 
But once Mr. Ondrik reached court it seemed clear that one fundamental, the assumption of guilt, had changed hardly at all.
By the end of the trial Mr. Goodwin had concluded that challenging the state case in a criminal prosecution here is largely fruitless, ''because the Government cannot be seen to lose.''
Rather than deciding innocence or guilt, he said, the court's function appeared to be ''assessing degrees of responsibility,'' a task it addressed at length in the Ondrik case by reviewing the deficiencies in the hotel's fire equipment and the bungling that ensued once the blaze broke out. 
Two Chinese, the hotel supervisor and the absentee floor attendant, face trials of their own for ''dereliction of duty.''
For his part, Mr. Ondrik seems to have resigned himself to a conviction. 
''It is difficult for me to accept that I am a criminal,'' he told the judges in his final plea. 
''This is partly because the law in my country and in China toward accidents is different. 
However, I am a friend of China and a guest in your country, and if this court decides that vengeance and punishment are necessary according to your laws, then I will accept that.''
