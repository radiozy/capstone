
Every day, many times a day, a specialized team of workers on a mission leaves a building in the shadows of the National Stadium.
Equipped with identical empty navy-blue rolling suitcases, they hop in cars, catch planes, ride in buses and trains, and occasionally squeeze onto three-wheeled scooters to reach their assignments. 
Some head to the nearby national sports training center. 
Others are off to far-flung locations in China, like the high-altitude training camps on the Tibetan plateau.
The workers, about 300 of them, are employed by the China Anti-Doping Agency to collect urine and blood samples from the country's best athletes. 
The samples are placed in the suitcases and transported here to China's main drug-testing lab, a $10 million facility that opened in November.
The job is not glamorous, but, to the Chinese, the mission is critical to the success of the Beijing Olympics; the opening ceremony is Friday. 
The International Olympic Committee has been in charge of the testing since July 27, and will be through the end of the Games, but officials here have gone out of their way to weed out cheats before they compete for China.
It would be devastating to the entire nation, high-level sports officials here said, if an athlete from the home country were to fail a drug test while the world was watching.
''From every level of our government, we have a very clear goal for our Chinese athletes, and that is for no positive doping cases to occur during the Olympics,'' Du Lijun, the director of the antidoping agency, said at the lab in Beijing. 
''This agency doesn't care about how many gold medals we have. 
We've been working very hard to cleanse China's name after some really bad situations ruined China's image.
''The most important thing for us is to maintain a good image.''
Four years ago, the Athens Games were marred by scandal when the Greek sprinters Katerina Thanou and Konstantinos Kenteris evaded drug testers by faking a motorcycle accident on the eve of the Olympics. 
They were barred from the Games.
Leading to the Beijing Olympics, 11 Greek and 11 Bulgarian weight lifters tested positive for banned substances. 
Seven Russian track and field athletes, including five Olympians, were suspended for substituting someone else's urine for their own for drug tests.
In January, Russian rowing federation administrators, coaches and medical personnel were barred from the sport for one year after nine rowers in the previous 12 months had been suspended for doping violations.
''I don't think any country is immune,'' David Howman, the director general of the World Anti-Doping Agency, said. 
''China went through periods of doping problems in the '90s. 
In the old days, it was East Germany. 
The U.S. has had its problems lately. 
No country wants a national athlete to test positive and spoil it.''
In the 1990s, several Chinese athletes virtually came out of nowhere to produce spectacular performances. 
More than 30 swimmers failed tests at international competitions. 
A Chinese swimmer headed to the 1998 world championships was caught with 13 vials of human growth hormone, a banned drug for which there will be widespread testing for the first time at these Games.
About the same time, a group of female runners coached by Ma Junren raised eyebrows. 
Ma said he fed his runners turtle soup and caterpillar fungus to increase their endurance, resulting in startling, world-record performances. 
But in 2000, six of his runners were kicked off the team for the Sydney Games because blood tests indicated they were using the blood-boosting hormone EPO. 
A test for that drug made its debut at those Olympics.
More recently, an investigation into steroids production led to more than 120 arrests in September in the United States, with law enforcement officials saying that they had traced 99 percent of the confiscated raw drugs to Chinese manufacturers.
The Chinese say they are redoubling their efforts to rebuild their reputation. 
After the swimmer Ouyang Kunpeng and the wrestler Luo Meng tested positive for banned drugs in the weeks before the Games, China warned other athletes. 
Ouyang, Luo and their coaches were barred for life from their sports, a much harsher penalty than called for by China's antidoping code or the World Anti-Doping Code, both of which mandate a two-year ban for a first violation. 
Chinese officials said the severe penalty was meant to deter potential cheats.
In fact, the Chinese may have been too strict. 
Howman said the World Anti-Doping Agency would consider appealing the bans of Ouyang, Luo and their coaches because the punishment did not comply with the agency's code.
''We owe a duty to all the athletes to come under the jurisdiction of the code to make sure the rules are followed,'' he said. 
''I would suggest that China is doing everything possible to ensure everything is clean, but we don't want to have different penalties per country.''
The Chinese government signed antidoping regulations into law in 2004. 
It made agreements with athletes and provinces, putting in writing that everyone involved in doping -- including athletes, doctors and manufacturers -- would pay a heavy price.
The China Anti-Doping Agency said it conducted 5,007 tests this year on China's top athletes; eight athletes, including six on the provincial level, tested positive.
''The Olympics are a lifelong opportunity for every athlete, so I'm sure someone is trying to cheat on us, no matter how harsh the punishment,'' Zhao Jian, the deputy director of China's antidoping agency, said. 
''Our job is to spare no effort in being even smarter in our testing schemes to catch people.''
About 70 percent of China's tests were done out of competition, when athletes are most likely to use banned substances. 
But China allows athletes an hour's notice, agency officials said, which could be enough time to manipulate a sample.
''Dopers would know very well how to escape detection in this amount of time,'' said Christiane Ayotte, the director of the laboratory outside Montreal that will help run the drug-testing program for the 2010 Vancouver Games. 
She added that the athletes could drink enough water to dilute their urine sufficiently to avoid testing positive.
Chinese officials are trying to prevent athletes from doping in the first place. 
The State General Administration of Sport has an education and propaganda office, which tells athletes and coaches about the dangers of performance-enhancing drugs. 
The Beijing Games organizing committee also financed an exhibit about doping that has toured China since January 2007. 
The program was to educate people involved in sports, but also regular citizens -- particularly the parents of athletes -- about doping.
''Sometimes, the parents say, 'You look really good, you have more muscles,' when their children may be doping,'' Zhao, of the antidoping agency, said of athletes' families. 
''They just don't know the signs.''
Yet athletes and coaches still cheat, and some do so on a wide scale. 
In a case reported worldwide, officials at a track and field school in Anshan, in northeastern China, were charged with doping their athletes in August 2006.
The headmaster and the staff were fired after the staff was found injecting drugs into athletes ages 15 to 18. 
More than 450 vials of steroids were found at the school. 
Authorities were tipped off by an anonymous caller to China's antidoping hotline.
The antidoping agency said this case showed that those who cheat would be caught. 
But the scandal also shed light on the seedy side of athletics in China, andthat wound is still raw.
In Anshan, a depressed iron and steel town dotted with billowing smokestacks, sports officials and the school's new administration bristle at the mention of doping.
This spring, when reporters at Anshan's stadium asked athletes about the scandal, a guard growled, ''No!'' and slammed a gate.
Li Gang, the owner of a sports shop across the street, spoke before a city sports official arrived to tell people not to talk to reporters.
''The doping here has embarrassed this city and the entire province,'' Li said. 
''Anyone related to it lost face and disappeared from here. 
It is so bad for us because we are the heart of track and field in this country.''
The Chinese are crossing their fingers that their athletes will give them no reason to be ashamed during the Olympics. 
Antidoping experts like Dick Pound, the former chairman of the World Anti-Doping Agency, seem hopeful.
''I told them that they had to go the extra mile, and they did,'' Pound said of his visit to China last year to discuss the country's antidoping plan with officials. 
''I said: 'You have to do more than other countries. 
You have to dispel the myths that you are hiding your athletes in Mongolia somewhere, out of the reach of the drug testers.'
''If a bunch of athletes no one has ever heard of show up at the Olympics and win gold medals, that's going to be the worst thing for China's reputation. 
I told them to fix it, and I think they did.''
