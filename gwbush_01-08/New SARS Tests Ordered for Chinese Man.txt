
Perplexed medical experts from China's Ministry of Health and the World Health Organization have ordered new rounds of tests for a man suspected of having SARS because previous results have been so contradictory that they cannot reach a diagnosis.
"We can't confirm that the patient is a SARS patient or that he is not," said Dr. Julie Hall, the SARS team leader in the organization's office in Beijing. 
She said his test results have "really been a mixed bag of negatives and positives."
On Tuesday, Dr. Hall said the Ministry of Health agreed to allow W.H.O. laboratories outside China to conduct additional tests on blood and other samples from the patient. 
She said the samples could be shipped out as soon as Wednesday, though she did not yet know when the testing would occur or at which labs.
New tests are also planned at the provincial health department in the southern city of Guangzhou, where the sick man is hospitalized, and at two laboratories in Beijing affiliated with the Chinese Center for Disease Control. 
Dr. Hall said those tests could be delayed a few days as researchers await a shipment of control materials.
The decision to conduct more tests came after a long Tuesday meeting in Beijing between Chinese and W.H.O. experts, and also followed a confusing day in which a provincial health official in Guangzhou told a news agency that test results had caused the upgrading of the case from suspected to confirmed.
"The case has been confirmed," Feng Shaoming, the health official, told Agence France-Presse. 
"Our experts at the Center for Disease Control have made many tests and they are all positive."
But by early Tuesday evening, W.H.O. officials said that it was still too soon to give a diagnosis on the case and that it remained unconfirmed. 
The Ministry of Health said in a daily update on its Web site the status of the case was unchanged.
The handling of this case is being watched particularly closely given the harsh criticism directed at China for its early handling of the original SARS outbreak. 
The first person believed infected by SARS came from a suburb of Guangzhou in November 2002. 
Government officials initially covered up the existence of the disease as it spread through the population.
Only after the disease spread first to Hong Kong and Beijing, then throughout the world, did the Chinese government acknowledge its mistakes and begin publicly reporting the outbreak.
Now, government officials are promising openness and swift action on any new cases. 
They have ordered that all suspected or confirmed cases be reported and made public. 
Provincial officials who attempt to hide the disease face heavy punishment.
On Tuesday, the Ministry of Health announced that a new reporting system for diseases like plague, cholera, AIDS and SARS would be instituted in 2004 to track occurrence rates and to provide information about public health emergencies.
The sick man in Guangzhou is a 32-year-old freelance television journalist, surnamed Luo, who first began having a fever on Dec. 
16. State media reported that Mr. Luo checked into a hospital on Dec. 
20 after a doctor suspected he had pneumonia in one lung. 
On Dec. 
24, he transferred to another hospital and was placed under quarantine. 
The case became public on Dec. 
26 when a newspaper in Guangzhou broke the story.
The patient's health is apparently improving; Dr. Hall said he had not had a fever, a telltale symptom of SARS, for several days.
Dr. Hall said investigators have not been able to determine where or when Mr. Luo could have come into contact with the disease.
Dr. Hall said none of the 81 people identified as having contact with him exhibited any symptoms. 
Of those, 42 people considered to have close contact remain quarantined. 
Their temperatures are tested every day, and they have submitted blood for testing. 
The other 39 people had casual contact with Mr. Luo, and some of those people have already been released from quarantine. 
Dr. Hall also denied a report in the Chinese media that Mr. Luo's girlfriend had shown SARS symptoms.
"His girlfriend is fine," she said.
