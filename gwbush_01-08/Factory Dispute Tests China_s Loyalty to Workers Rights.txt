
As many as 2,000 former workers from a closed brick and tile factory in the Chinese province of Inner Mongolia have clashed with the police, and hundreds are occupying the factory, demanding pension benefits.
The confrontation involves a common theme of industrial disputes these days in China: what, if any, pensions and other benefits are owed to longtime workers at state-owned factories that are closed or sold to investors?
There have been hundreds of such disputes in recent years as China has moved toward a market economy. 
But most end peacefully and without arrests.
On Monday, the workers tried to march to city government offices in Baotou, 340 miles west of Beijing, one worker said. 
The police and militia blocked their path and, after a standoff lasting most of the day, arrested three workers and a bystander, said the worker, who gave only his surname, Liu, in a telephone interview from the occupied factory.
About half the workers returned to the factory, Mr. Liu said. 
While some workers have since wearied of the confrontation and left, several hundred are still inside, he said, adding that many tried this morning to block a nearby rail route but were driven back by police.
Calls to the company's offices were not answered; Mr. Liu said that workers had blocked the entrance to its offices with bricks and scrap on Tuesday and that no one was inside today. 
About 200 police and 700 militia armed with riot shields and electric prods had taken up positions outside the factory, he said.
An office director at the district government of Donghe, an administrative area on Baotou's eastern outskirts, the site of the factory, confirmed the clash.
He accused the workers of having resorted to violence. 
"The nature of this protest is changed," said the director, who declined to give his name. 
"They beat the police. 
They blocked the road for 12 hours and seriously undermined the public order."
Mr. Liu denied that workers had beaten police officers.
Such episodes of worker unrest have not become so serious that they appear to threaten the country's political stabilityII, but they pose a serious problem for a Communist Party that still bases its official ideology partly on representing the interests of workers.
Mr. Liu said that his factory had gone bankrupt a few years ago. 
Its owner, the Baotou municipal government, turned it into a private company, the Changzheng Building Materials Corporation. 
Workers were given a small stake.
But the company, which has operated the factory for a few weeks out of the last year and a half, wants to turn much the site into an industrial park.
Mr. Liu said that the factory's pension fund, worth $1 million, was missing, leaving workers with nothing in a society in which people are increasingly expected to fend for themselves.
The occupation of the factory, "won't end until we get our money back," Mr. Liu said.
But the Donghe government office director said that workers should not turn to the government for help because Changzheng was a private company.
"It's an independent legal entity," he said, "and should be taking care of itself on its own and solving its problem on its own."
