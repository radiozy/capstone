
I'M clacking away at my computer on this sultry summer afternoon when I have to pause. 
There's an unfamiliar ambience that's settled onto my house. 
If memory serves me correctly, people call it ''quiet.''
Around here, it's characterized by a lack of giggling, door slamming, SpongeBob cackling, computer-game blaring, car-horn honking and yelling between floors. 
My study is so tranquil I can hear myself think. 
It's unnatural. 
It's disorienting. 
But most of all, it's bliss.
Anyone who has kids knows that serenity like this doesn't just happen. 
In my case, it comes after months spent filling out contact info sheets, running down obscure outdoor survival items, arranging wellness check-ups at the pediatrician and otherwise acting as a travel agent for my three children. 
Now I'm enjoying the fruits of my labors: a vacation from parenthood while they're on various summer adventures -- a temporary empty nest. 
Because of rolling start times, their absences overlap by 16 days, 5 hours and 30 minutes. 
But who's counting?
My kids certainly aren't. 
By all accounts, they're having a blast, and why wouldn't they?
When I was growing up in Missouri, a camping trip with your peers meant a two-day bus tour of Silver Dollar City. 
But my children are already so well traveled that I can only aspire to one day be as accomplished at globe-trotting as they are. 
At 10, Selena's in her second year of sleep-away camp in central Vermont; Jay, 14, is doing community service on the Caribbean island of Dominica; and Ted, 17, is in a cultural exchange program in southwestern China, happily dining on unusual animal parts.
And yet, to me, what my husband, Harry, and I are experiencing while they're gone is far more exotic. 
Within nine months of our marriage 19 years ago, we were expecting Ted, and I can't even remember a time when our social lives didn't hinge on the whims of teenage baby sitters.
At this point, before Ted leaves for college and the nest starts emptying out for real, a limited taste of freedom is delicious. 
We can experience life as just a couple and their dog, one who's never embarrassed to be seen with us, and whose eternal devotion can be bought off with a little stroking and the occasional bacon-flavored biscuit.
Since my kids have been gone, I've viscerally experienced the cliche about absence making the heart grow fonder. 
I infinitely adore them when they're too far away to ask for money or rides all over town. 
When I think of Ted helping his ancient Chinese host grandmother cook dumplings, or picture Jay helping to paint a community center for local Carib families, they're the perfect children. 
I envision them slumbering with angelic smiles on their faces, as opposed to pouting to sleep because they can't borrow their father's laptop.
Not that Harry and I have been the models of good behavior. 
After nearly 18 years of having to set the right examples, we're thrilled to practice some bad ones. 
We have thrown-together sandwiches for dinner in front of the TV news, feet sprawled all over the furniture, and we don't even use plates. 
We drink beer during the week. 
We leave junk mail and clothes piled up on our bureaus. 
We eat too much candy, and don't brush our teeth for hours. 
We parade around the house naked, just because we can.
Harry doesn't feel pressure to make it home in time for the family dinner, and as a freelance writer, I can experience the joy of total self-involvement. 
I sleep later than I should and always do errands, such as walking the dog in the nearby reservation or grocery shopping, after 4 p.m., when my daughter's bus would be coming home during the school year and I'd be effectively grounded.
We've gotten reservations at sushi-joints-of-the-moment with similarly footloose couples, and never once checked our watches. 
The words ''baby sitter'' haven't crossed our minds or our lips.
On weekends, we've packed up and traveled light (compared with a vacationing family of five, the circus travels light). 
We've bunked at our friends' Dewey Beach, Del., home and, as we skipped out toward the waves, smiled sympathetically at them as they performed the half-hour ritual of preparing their daughters to battle the UV rays. 
We booked a last-minute three-day weekend to Lake Placid, where my husband and dog shimmied up mountains while I shopped and slept. 
Everyone was happy.
Since Lake Placid has always been our favorite family destination, it did cross my mind that Selena would have loved to have picked some of the blueberries we popped into our mouths while climbing Owl's Head, a small peak on the way into town. 
I also thought wistfully about the first time we had visited, when Ted was 4 months old. 
In those days, eating out meant tag-teaming as one parent paced in the parking lot with a howling baby in a snuggly, and the other wolfed down Mexican fast food as quickly as humanly possible.
When the remnants of a hurricane washed out our Saturday afternoon in the North Country, I reveled in being able to read, but the weather made me reflect on how wet vacation days used to bring the family together for some creative tourism. 
Over the years, we drove to Fort Ticonderoga and Canada, watched Olympic skaters pirouette and took in spectacular waterfalls. 
We shared the joy of discovery, and one another's company.
It also occurred to me that before I know it, I'll be dangling all manner of incentives -- free laundry, meals, car rides, the very services my kids rely upon now -- to lure them back home so we can all be in the same place at the same time, sharing the same view. 
Quiet and tranquillity are great, and so is raucous, tumultuous togetherness, as long as you can alternate them every once in a while. 
With a few more days left to savor the silence, I can plan how to celebrate when my kids return and fill the nest again. 
And maybe devote a couple of hours to thinking about where they can go next summer.
