
In a policy trial, China will release untraded stock in four state-controlled companies to the market, hoping to gauge the impact before freeing a much greater amount of state-owned holdings over time.
The announcement on Monday by the Securities Regulatory Commission followed another one quietly made last week. 
In that statement, the government said it would aim to eventually place all its nontradable stockholdings -- about $300 billion at current prices -- on the market. 
Such a move would, in effect, privatize all of China's listed companies.
On Monday, however, the regulators started with only a small parcel of stock on an experimental basis.
The four companies in question -- the Shanghai Zi Jiang Enterprise Group, a packaging maker; Sany Heavy Industry, which makes machinery; Tsinghua Tongfang, a computer company; and the Hebei Jinniu Energy Resources Company, a coal company -- have a combined market capitalization of about $2.4 billion, or about 1 percent of China's total.
The regulators made the announcement before the start of trading Monday, and the news sent China's main exchanges in Shanghai and Shenzhen to six-year lows as investors feared dilution of their stock. 
The Shanghai index dropped 2.4 percent and the Shenzhen slumped 3.3 percent.
Some Chinese investors criticized the plan as the latest of a string of official missteps that have hobbled China's stock markets as the country's economy has soared.
Others, however, guardedly welcomed the move as a painful but necessary step in addressing factors that have sent Chinese share prices into a tailspin.
Investors have cited widespread corruption, managerial incompetence and the cooling effects of government control of the state-owned companies that crowd the country's stock exchanges as reasons for the slump.
''These reforms may help in the longer term, but in the shorter term investors may taste bitter medicine,'' said He Jun, director of research at the Anbound Group, an investment consultant. 
''This reform isn't going to solve all the share markets' problems, but without this sort of change you're never going to solve any of those problems.''
Most of the 1,000 or so companies listed in China are state-owned. 
Typically, these companies sell a minority stake to the public, leaving most of the equity, and effective control, in government hands. 
About two-thirds of listed Chinese companies' value is locked up as nontradable state-held equity, analysts estimate.
''China's domestic stock markets aren't at all pure markets,'' said Ai Qunce, chief executive of International Credit, a Chinese investment company. 
''The government has used them to park many badly performing state-owned companies, and so there's always been a fundamental distortion between China's economic performance and the performance of the stock market.''
The merging of market investment with state control has often failed to improve the companies' performance, while state-appointed managers' access to investor capital has led to widespread corruption that has battered market confidence, Mr. He said. 
''Violations in the marketplace simply aren't monitored and swiftly punished,'' he said.
But analysts said that China faced a difficult situation: on the one hand, it needs to sell its holdings to raise cash for its social welfare and pension programs; on the other, the government fears inundating the country's stock markets with huge amounts of equity at a time the exchanges are already struggling. 
A previous effort to unload state holdings, in 2001, was abandoned after stock prices fell by a third.
But the transfer of state assets to openly traded markets will ultimately discourage the pillaging of state-owned companies and the manipulating of stock prices, said Ivan Chung, managing director of Xinhua Finance, a credit rating company that monitors listed companies.
''It's a step forward for privatization and will inject more transparency,'' he said. 
''Shareholders will be able to have more confidence in management.''
Expanding the policy to huge state conglomerates, like Baoshan Iron and Steel and Sinopec, the oil company, however, is likely to jar sensitive political nerves and would require high-level political navigation, as many officials would be reluctant to give up their lucrative leverage over state assets, Mr. Chung said.
Other investors said that government interference in the stock market was bound to foil this latest resuscitation attempt. 
''These reforms will inevitably fail, just like the last ones and the ones before that,'' said Zhang Weixing, the chief economist of AICC, an investment company.
Mr. Zhang said investors were not consulted about the latest reforms, leaving delicate choices in the hands of officials. 
''We're desperately ill and need surgery by experts, but the government wants bureaucrats to be brain surgeons,'' he said.
