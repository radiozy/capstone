
New Boston Review: Why did you decide to write about photography?
Susan Sontag: Because I've had the experience of being obsessed by photographs. 
And because virtually all the important aesthetic, moral and political problems -- the question of ''modernity'' itself and of ''modernist'' taste -- are played out in photography's relatively brief history.
William K. Ivins has called the camera the most important invention since the printing press. 
For the evolution of sensibility, the invention of the camera is perhaps even more important. 
It is, of course, the uses to which photography is put in our culture, in the consumer society, that make photography so interesting and so potent. 
In the People's Republic of China, people don't see ''photographically.''
The Chinese take pictures of each other and of famous sites and monuments, as we do. 
But they're baffled by the foreigner who will rush to take a picture of an old, battered, peeling farmhouse door. 
They don't have our idea of the ''picturesque.''
They don't understand photography as a method of appropriating and transforming reality -- in pieces -- which denies the very existence of inappropriate or unworthy subject matter. 
As a current ad for the Polaroid SX-70 puts it: ''It won't let you stop. 
Suddenly you see a picture everywhere you look.''
N.B.R.: Do you see ''photographically''?
Sontag: Of course.
N.B.R.: Do you take photographs?
Sontag: I don't own a camera. 
I'm a photograph junkie, but I don't want to take them.
N.B.R.: Why?
Sontag: Perhaps I might really get hooked.
