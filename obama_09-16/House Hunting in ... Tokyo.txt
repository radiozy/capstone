
A TWO-BEDROOM ONE-BATH APARTMENT IN CENTRAL TOKYO
66 MILLION YEN (ABOUT $740,000)
This apartment is in the Hiroo neighborhood in the Shibuya ward of Tokyo. 
Last renovated in April 2008, it has 82.8 square meters (about 890 square feet), with new hardwood floors and wallpaper, and a new kitchen and bath. 
The windows face southeast, allowing for sunlight all day. 
The tree-lined views from the master bedroom and living room are of Shoun-ji Temple, a Zen Buddhist center. 
The apartment is one of 11 units in a five-story 1978 building.
The living area is open to the kitchen and dining area; taken together the space has about 35 square meters (about 380 square feet). 
Off the living room is a storage balcony running nearly the length of the apartment. 
The kitchen has a pantry area, and new recessed and pendant light fixtures.
The two bedrooms are roughly of equal size, around 15 square meters (160 square feet); each has a closet, and one has a sliding door opening onto the patio. 
A full bath off the entrance foyer has an above-counter vessel sink and a mirror surrounded by royal blue laminate tiles; there is a combination shower/bathtub with a digital water temperature interface. 
Appliances and furniture can be included in the selling price upon request.
The unit is a short walk from both Hiroo's main shopping street and the Hiroo station of Tokyo's Metro; there are international schools, embassies and stores in the immediate area, including the popular National Azabu supermarket. 
Arisugawa Memorial Park is also nearby, with a pond, trails and tennis courts.
MARKET OVERVIEW
Japan's persistent economic problems have made themselves felt in the local residential property market. 
In January, the country's Real Estate Economic Institute reported that sales of condominiums had dropped 9.9 percent from the same period last year; over all, according to the institute, the average price of a new condo was 45.35 million yen ($510,000), about 5 percent lower than a year ago.
''The Tokyo real estate market has declined since the Lehman shock by 20 to 40 percent,'' said Zoe Ward, an agent in the sales division of Housing Japan, which is listing the property described here. 
''However, we think it has hit the bottom.''
In 2009, according to the institute, the number of condominiums on the market in Tokyo went below 40,000 units for the first time since 1992 -- though the number is expected to increase this year. 
Sumitomo Realty and Development, a national Japanese real estate company, reported that its own transaction prices were down 21 percent in 2009.
''One of the reasons why it's so slow to pick up is continuous negative behavior of Japanese banks toward loans,'' said Kazuhiro Okuda, president of Tokyo's Hikari Home Inc., a real estate company that rents and sells properties to foreigners, referring to the reticence of Japanese banks in issuing mortgages.
Nonetheless, home prices in central Tokyo still exceed those of many other major world markets. 
Prices range from 700,000 to 1.4 million yen per square meter ($730 to $1,460 per square foot), Ms. Ward said; the average size of a unit is about 70 square meters (750 square feet).
For new construction, Mr. Okuda said, prices range from 1.4 million to 1.8 million yen per square meter ($1,460 to $1,880 per square foot); Ms. Ward says the upper value can extend up to 2.1 million yen per square meter ($2,200 per square foot). 
The apartment described here, at nearly 83 square meters, is about 795,000 yen ($8,930) per square meter, or $830 per square foot.
WHO BUYS IN TOKYO
While fewer bankers have moved to Tokyo since the fall of Lehman Brothers in 2008, said Kentaro Ohashi, an agent at Prime Property Tokyo, there are still plenty of foreigners in other fields: Americans, French, Germans and Britons.
At Relo Japan, a company that provides moving services to expatriates, business of late has come mainly from the United States, with some from Australia and Britain, said James Keane, a relocation consultant with the company. 
And Chinese buyers are becoming a larger presence in the local market, with real estate seminars now being held for potential Chinese buyers in Tokyo, The Straits Times of Singapore reported last month.
BUYING BASICS
Foreign buyers do not face any major barriers, Ms. Ward said. 
On closing day, the purchase is subject to a variety of fees: a notary fee, typically 50,000 to 200,000 yen ($560 to $2,250); a registration license tax of about 1 percent of the value of the building and the land, payable through a notary; a stamp tax, which ranges from 15,000 to 200,000 yen ($170 to $2,250) based on the property purchase price; and fees for real estate agents, which Ms. Ward estimated at 3.15 percent, on top of a fixed charge of 60,000 yen ($670). 
The fees will total a minimum of several thousand yen beyond the closing price of a home.
When looking for property in Tokyo, it's important to distinguish between freehold and leasehold, Ms. Ward said. 
Freehold units include ownership of the land on which the apartment building is situated, and thus command a premium over leasehold properties. 
The unit described here is freehold, which entitles the owner to about 28 square meters (about 300 square feet) of the building's total 275.32 square meters of land (2,960 square feet).
Most foreign buyers look for freehold properties, Ms. Ward said. 
In such transactions the value of the land itself can constitute up to 90 percent of the asking price for older units; if a developer wishes to buy a building, all the owners must agree. 
But property seekers who opt for leasehold often get more living space for their money. 
Leases usually last 50 to 60 years, though some new-construction units have renewable leases, she added.

TAXES AND FEES
Taxes in 2009 were 128,600 yen (about $1,440); the tax is recalculated each year based on an evaluation of the building and land.
The management fee for the apartment is 36,009 yen ($400) a month; the restoration fee is 25,720 yen ($300) a month.
A security system, which comes with English-language support, costs 3,150 yen ($35) a month. 
The owner can also buy fire and earthquake insurance, which ranges from 30,000 to 100,000 yen ($335 to $1,100), based on the degree of coverage.
The stamp tax on the apartment is about 105,000 yen (about $1,180). 
The registration license tax is about 354,800 yen (about $4,000). 
The notary fee is around 100,000 yen (about $1,120). 
There is no broker's fee, as Housing Japan is the direct seller.
