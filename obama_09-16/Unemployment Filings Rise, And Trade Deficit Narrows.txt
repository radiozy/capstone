
Economic data released Thursday showed an increase in jobless filings last week, an unexpected narrowing of the trade deficit in November and a sharp rise in wholesale prices in December.
The Labor Department said that more people applied for unemployment benefits last week after retailers shed temporary holiday employees.
The number of people seeking benefits jumped by 35,000 to a seasonally adjusted 445,000 for the week ending Jan. 
8. It was the highest level since late October. 
The spike pushed up the four-week average, a less volatile measure, to 416,500. 
The average had fallen to a two-year low the previous week.
In another report, the Commerce Department said America's trade deficit edged down to the lowest point in 10 months as exports, helped by a weaker dollar and rising foreign demand, climbed to the highest level in more than two years.
The trade deficit narrowed to $38.3 billion in November, down 0.3 percent from October's revised $38.4 billion deficit.
Through the first 11 months of 2010, the deficit was running at an annual rate of $500.4 billion, 33.5 percent higher than in 2009, a year when the recession cut into Americans' appetite for imports.
For November, exports were up 0.8 percent to $159.6 billion, the highest point since August 2008. 
The increase was led by higher sales of commercial aircraft, computers and farm products like corn and wheat.
Imports had a smaller gain, increasing 0.6 percent to $198 billion, reflecting a 6.7 percent jump in petroleum imports, which climbed to $27.2 billion.
The trade deficit with China, the largest with any country, rose 0.5 percent to $25.6 billion in November and is on track to set a one-year record. 
Through November, the deficit with China was running at an annual rate of $275.3 billion, which would exceed the previous record of $268 billion set in 2008.
For November, exports to China reached a monthly record of $9.5 billion but imports from China rose as well, climbing to $35.1 billion.
On the wholesale level, prices in December logged their biggest increase in nearly a year, lifted by more expensive energy and food costs. 
But most other prices were steady, suggesting inflation was not spreading through the economy.
The Producer Price Index, which measures price changes before they reach consumers, rose 1.1 percent in December, up from a 0.8 percent rise in November. 
It was the largest increase since January 2010.
But outside the volatile energy and food categories, all other prices rose 0.2 percent, down from a 0.3 percent rise. 
The lower reading on core prices indicates inflation is not breaking out.
For the 12 months ending December, wholesale prices rose 4 percent. 
That was down from a 4.3 percent rise in 2009.
