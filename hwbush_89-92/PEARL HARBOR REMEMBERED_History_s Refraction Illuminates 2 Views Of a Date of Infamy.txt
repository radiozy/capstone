
On Japan's airwaves this week, the battleship Arizona has sunk a dozen times, the aging Zero pilots have been interviewed so many times that dedicated viewers know their exploits by heart, and a stream of commentators have talked -- and talked, and talked -- about how the United States and Japan must seize this weekend to reaffirm the most critical alliance linking East and West.
But in subtle and telling ways, the images and explanations being offered here and in the United States to explain how two great powers came to battle each other are strikingly different.
For many Japanese, this has been a long week of trying, once again, to find comfort in a carefully sculpted interpretation of history that overarches the country's memories of the war. 
In dramas and documentaries, the Japanese have watched this week as their fathers and mothers made a futile effort to steer the country away from war, as a small group of military leaders drove a peaceful people to ruin, and as their country rose from its defeat to be as technologically powerful as the nation to whom it eventually surrendered.
Perhaps the clearest cleavage of interpretations came in the studios of NHK, the giant semiofficial broadcaster, which struggled for months with producers from ABC over a documentary that was aired this week in both countries. 
Although American and Japanese viewers saw nearly identical film, they heard very different scripts, and they were differences that went beyond the imprecision of translations.
 
2 Views of Rape of Nanking
In an 18-second clip about Japan's 1937 capture of Nanking, the then capital of China, ABC reported that "Japan's claim it was liberating China was ludicrous to Americans, particularly when hundreds of thousands of Chinese men, women and babies were murdered in what was called the Rape of Nanking. 
The U.S. sent China $50 million in war relief."
But in Japan, where the Rape of Nanking is not mentioned in textbooks and the country's right wing contends that the incident was a fabrication, viewers heard nothing about war atrocities.
Though NHK's version of the documentary showed vivid footage of Chinese being shot in a ditch, the voice-over said simply, "In a totalitarian attempt to grab land and wealth, the Japanese military had led the country into an eight-year war in Manchuria and China," an invasion that caused tension with the United States.
At a luncheon with foreign correspondents on Friday, Mikio Kawaguchi, the chairman of NHK, said there had been no effort to rewrite history for a Japanese audience, simply an attempt to explain things differently to American and Japanese viewers, who "have a different way of looking at history and life."
"Even though both sides were sincere in their efforts, the methods they wanted to employ to portray what they saw as truth were different," Mr. Kawaguchi said of his company's joint venture with the American network. 
"There were perception gaps. 
The two sides had such disagreements that they feared they might be headed into a second Pearl Harbor."
 
A Weeklong History Lesson
Without question, Japan learned a lot of history this week. 
Many young people said that until the flurry of programs, they had not been certain why Pearl Harbor prompted such strong emotions in America. 
At the same time, the tone of much of the coverage paralleled the tone of the little that is taught about the war in schools: Japan was more a victim than an aggressor.
Several broadcasts explored the internment of Japanese-Americans in the United States. 
Several reported arguments that President Roosevelt had known about the attack in advance and done nothing to stop it. 
Others found a simple answer for Japan's ultimate defeat: The country was simply outproduced by the United States. 
A lengthy program today, "The Secret Story of the War Over Steel," showed how the giant American steelmakers that provided the hardware for Japan's defeat later fell to Japanese competition.
Japanese television warmed to another popular theme here: that the Japanese military, rather than the Japanese people or even political leaders, was solely responsible for plunging the nation into war.
On Friday night, for example, viewers tuned into a vivid drama called "Defeat in Showa 16," a title suggesting that Japan lost the war as soon as it began in 1941. 
The Japanese calendar begins anew with the ascension of each new emperor, and 1941 was the 16th year of the Showa era, Emperor Hirohito's reign.
 
Shouting at the War Institute
Based on fact, the drama was the story of a railway executive, a bureaucrat and other Japanese who were drafted into the Government's "Total War Research Institute."
The institute was charged with determining what would happen if the country gave everything -- from its steel works to its samurai spirit -- to the cause of "liberating" the country from encirclement by Dutch, British and American colonialists.
In the show, the young men of the institute concluded that the Government's war plans were madness. 
There were frequent shouting matches with the military brass, but the young men's voices were drowned out. 
Many historians and older Japanese say that the spirit of such dramas is accurate.
"Everyone knew that relations were at a critical position, even though there was no indication war would be coming so soon," said Junnosuke Ofusa, who joined The New York Times bureau in Tokyo in 1930 and helped guide the newspaper's coverage of Japan for the next five decades. 
Mr. Ofusa, who retired several years ago, said that "no one said outwardly that it was a bad idea, because the military police were everywhere. 
But the Japanese people knew there was little chance of winning the war."
At the same time, some Japanese bridle at placing all of the blame on the military, because the argument is often used to absolve the rest of the country.
"Of course the military had much of the power," Seiki Nishihiro, a former Deputy Defense Minister, said the other day, discussing the coverage of the anniversary. 
"But the mass media and everyone else followed, so not only the military was responsible."
Bubbling just beneath the surface of the coverage was the question of whether, and how, Japan should apologize for the Pearl Harbor attack.
Government spokesmen, who have spent much of the last year fretting about the anniversary celebrations, were openly distraught. 
While they were issuing statements of "regret" or "remorse" or "self-reflection" -- a great deal of time was spent trying to figure out which word best described Japan's position -- Parliament was going in the other direction, scuttling any parliamentary apology altogether.
"Why should we fling mud at the history of Japan?"
Masayuki Fujio, a senior politician, asked the other day, in a remark that was quoted in the Japanese news media throughout the weekend.
But the week was so painful that this country, which planned no formal events to commemorate Pearl Harbor, seems eager to have the anniversary behind it. 
"In a sense it is a kind of Pearl Harbor week anyway," said a weary-looking Taizo Watanabe, the Foreign Ministry's chief spokesman. 
"So many questions asked, so many answers and apologies from us."
With one more apology on early Sunday morning here, after President Bush's address at Pearl Harbor, the Japanese hope it will be all over, so they can go back to topics that are easier to discuss.
"Ask me about anything else," a senior Government official said the other day. 
"Ask me why the trade deficit is getting bigger again. 
Ask me about why we are so slow to open the rice market. 
Just don't ask me about Pearl Harbor."
