
The Chinese press announced the ousting of President Mikhail S. Gorbachev promptly and factually on radio and television news programs on Monday, with no sign of the glee that some Chinese hard-liners may feel for their apparent reprieve from the time when their Soviet "Big Brother" in the Communist movement would slip into political pluralism and capitalism.
Chinese leaders have long appeared to feel a deep animosity for Mr. Gorbachev, based on the view that he has undermined Communist governments in other countries as well as his own. 
While Beijing did not publicly criticize Mr. Gorbachev, China's hard-liners have characterized him in conversations and internal documents as a dangerous revisionist who had betrayed Eastern Europe and abandoned Leninist principles.
"The changes in the Soviet Union are the internal affairs of that country, and China hopes that the Soviet people will solve their own problems by themselves and maintain political stabilityI," the Beijing Government said in a statement issued this morning.
"I think they're thrilled," Merle Goldman, a China scholar at Boston University who is visiting Beijing, said of the Chinese leaders. 
"It only proves that political reform leads to chaos and instability. 
They can just say, 'I told you so!'
"
Still, Chinese leaders may well worry about how events will develop in the Soviet Union and in particular that any Soviet unrest could spill across the border. 
For example, if the ouster of Mr. Gorbachev intensifies rather than eases the demands in the Islamic regions of the Soviet Union, that could also stir pro-independence feelings in the Islamic northwest of China.
