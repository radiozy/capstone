
CHINA'S CRISIS, CHINA'S HOPE
By Liu Binyan.
Harvard University, $22.50.
"Things are seldom what they seem," goes a song in "H.M.S. 
Pinafore," and no one should know that better than Liu Binyan, a former reporter for the Chinese Communist Party newspaper, People's Daily. 
Mr. Liu was one of the intellectuals expelled from the party in 1987; his longtime criticism of official corruption had made him a hero to many of his readers. 
He was repeatedly refused permission to travel to the United States to accept invitations tendered through the Nieman Foundation at Harvard University. 
Yet he was allowed to accept a 1988 invitation. 
Why?
Mr. Liu thinks this reflected, even in so small a gesture, a fundamental transformation in China -- the will of the people growing as a political force, despite party policies, aged leaders and political events. 
The essays in "China's Crisis, China's Hope" (nicely translated by Howard Goldblatt) are based on lectures Mr. Liu gave at Harvard's Fairbank Center for East Asian Research in 1988 and 1989, before the death of the Communist Party General Secretary Hu Yaobang, the eruption of the democracy movement and the June 1989 Tiananmen Square massacre. 
The author has included two additional essays and a preface written after those events, assessing the great changes he sees in Chinese society and politics. 
His picture is not overly rosy. 
China faces serious problems -- its arable land, for example, has decreased by 50 percent since 1949 -- and even the eventual removal from power of the current leadership will be only one step forward. 
Despite Mr. Liu's pessimism, the underlying sense of these essays is one of optimism for "the flood that is China's reforms."
