
After the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown, China's bold experiment in fusing socialism and capitalism in this South China city stalled. 
By last summer, Shenzhen's mayor lost his position, economic reforms were halted, and the city's 10th anniversary celebration was postponed.
But now Shenzhen has talked its way back into Beijing's favor, and many leaders again point to it as a model for the nation's development.
Shenzhen officials, though wary of the power struggles in Beijing, are seeking a nearly open border with Hong Kong. 
A primitive stock market that has operated, literally, over the counters in small buildings around the city is about to expand into a modern, computerized exchange.
In a single decade, Shenzhen has developed from a barren patch of grassland into one of China's most modern metropolises. 
But the transformation has been marked by a battle between China's ardent central planners and those who envision a national economy far more open and free.
Shenzhen, a special economic zone in Guangdong province, is a product of China's effort to prove that a capitalist economy can thrive under a socialist government. 
Many central officials still fear that Shenzhen is becoming too capitalist and too unwieldy for the Government to control.
Local leaders say they have strong reasons for continuing to support the Beijing Government, which has allowed Shenzhen and other special economic zones to prosper. 
While some of Shenzhen's students protested in May 1989, the demonstrations were much smaller than in other big cities, as many citizens saw that protests would only stand in the way of making money.
Some say Shenzhen is turning into a mini-Hong Kong. 
Already, the Hong Kong dollar is Shenzhen's semiofficial currency, accepted by hotels, taxis and restaurants, which refuse the foreign currency exchange certificates required of foreigners everywhere else in China.
 
Rapid Industrial Growth
Industrial growth over the last decade averaged 69 percent annually, among the fastest of any city in the world. 
For a while, Shenzhen's tiny, nascent stock market was more free-wheeling -- and scandal-ridden -- than Hong Kong's.
While some Shenzhen officials agree about the increasing resemblance to Hong Kong, they insist that there is one major distinction: Shenzhen is still socialist, with most businesses run by the state.
"It is not impossible for Shenzhen to become a Chinese Hong Kong," said Wang Xinkuo, a senior manager at the Catic Shenzhen Enterprise Group, whose member companies include a maker of simple electronic components. 
"The biggest difference is that Hong Kong is a capitalist system, while in Shenzhen, public ownership is the main path."
Besides Shenzhen, with nearly 1.9 million people, there are four other special economic zones -- Zhuhai, Shantou, Xiamen and Hainan Island, all also in southern China. 
While officials still describe these zones as socialist, not everyone agrees that socialist planning will dominate these export-oriented cities.
 
Prices Set by Market
Almost all their prices, including those for raw materials, are set by the market. 
The rest of China buys many materials, including coal and oil, at subsidized prices.
"Only postage and bus fares are set by the Government, and that's because they are uniform for the whole nation," said Liang Wensen, a professor of economics at Shenzhen University.
"There is even a market in people's labor," Mr. Lian said, with most of Shenzhen's wages also determined primarily by market forces.
Partly because of its location next to Hong Kong, Shenzhen has attracted major foreign investments. 
Some 3,000 of Shenzhen's 7,000 companies have some foreign capital. 
Total foreign investment in Shenzhen amounts to about one-seventh the foreign investment in all of China, according to official statistics.
 
A Bumpy Ascent
But Shenzhen's ascent has not been smooth. 
When China's economic liberals had the upper hand in the late 1980's, Shenzhen was developing into a virtually free market. 
But when hard-liners took over two years ago during the Tiananmen crackdown, it seemed that Shenzhen's best years had ended.
Beijing refused to allow Shenzhen's modernized stock exchange to open; Li Hao, a reformer, was relieved as mayor, although he remained party secretary, and the city's 10th anniversary celebration, scheduled for last summer, was put off for nearly four months.
But now, Shenzhen seems to be back on the reform track. 
Once more, national leaders tout Shenzhen as a model, and again provincial officials from throughout China are visiting to learn how to help their own economies. 
Shenzhen has adopted an aggressive, 10-year growth plan, and a new airport is scheduled to open in the fall.
Shenzhen's leaders are sufficiently confident of their futures to press for more dramatic changes. 
Shenzhen is said to have sought Beijing's permission to establish its own currency and relax border restrictions with Hong Kong while tightening them with the rest of China. 
Already, other Chinese need special permission to cross the "second border" into Shenzhen.
 
Beijing's Concerns
Shenzhen's leaders apparently want to merge their economy into Hong Kong's, but it is unclear how far the hard-liners in Beijing will go along.
The central Government worries that Shenzhen will become too self-sufficient or closely integrated with Hong Kong, widening its economic lead over the rest of China.
"By the year 2000, Shenzhen's per capita G.N.P. should reach or almost reach the present level of typically developed countries," said Zheng Liangyu, Shenzhen's new Mayor, in a recent report. 
"The more profoundly we reform, the more contradictions and problems we shall face. 
This thus requires us to explore and experiment more bravely than we did 10 years ago, so as to really act as the pioneer and testing ground."
The saga of Shenzhen's stock market exemplifies the perils of economic revamping. 
For nearly a year after the stock market opened at the end of 1989, workers, clerks, taxi drivers and other residents viewed buying stocks as a sure way to riches because the market only went up.
 
Began With 5 Stocks
The stock market started with only five stocks, and the Government limited daily price changes to 5 percent. 
A lively black market developed where investors traded stocks at large premiums over official prices.
With no comprehensive securities law, there were no restrictions on Shenzhen officials owning stock. 
Many had bought stocks both to promote the new market and to make profits.
But in October, rumors circulated that to avoid conflicts of interest, Chinese officials would soon be banned from owning stock, and stock prices would be frozen. 
Many officials apparently tried to dump their stocks, and the black market crashed. 
Shenzhen had its first stock market scandal.
 
City Is Rebuked
The scandal soon came to Beijing's attention. 
In late November, the Government cracked down on the black market and reduced the limits on daily price changes to five-tenths of 1 percent.
Officials said privately that Vice President Wang Zhen, a hard-liner, circulated a memo among top leaders that criticized the chaotic Shenzhen market.
In response, Prime Minister Li Peng dispatched a team from Beijing to investigate. 
In a rebuke to Shenzhen, the central Government decided to allow Shanghai to become the first Chinese city to open a modern, computerized securities exchange.
The Shanghai market opened in December, and officials say that Beijing has now also approved the opening of a unified Shenzhen exchange, which is preparing to list an additional 10 stocks. 
Shenzhen, it seems, is supposed to develop and prosper, but in an orderly manner that does not alarm the central planners.
Local officials have learned to be more tactful in discussing stock markets and other initiatives.
"For some time we considered stock exchanges as something capitalist, but that is not right," said Lei Yu, the deputy mayor of Guangzhou, the capital of Guangdong province, as he commented on Shenzhen's market. 
"It's just a good way to raise funds."
