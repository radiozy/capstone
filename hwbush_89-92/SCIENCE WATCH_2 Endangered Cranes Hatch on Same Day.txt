
TWO black-necked cranes, a rare species that lives in the wild only in remote parts of Central Asia, hatched in captivity on the same day but half a world apart. 
The hatchings on July 10 were the first in captivity outside China.
One was hatched at the International Crane Foundation in Baraboo, Wis., the other at Vogelpark, a bird sanctuary in Walsrode, West Germany. 
Another egg at Vogelpark is expected to hatch soon.
George Archibald, director of the Baraboo crane center, said of the chick there, ''It's just as healthy as can be, growing very rapidly.''
The black-necked crane, named after the coal black feathers on its neck, stands four to five feet tall when grown. 
The crane's piercing call can reach several miles. 
Only 1,615 are known to exist, making it one of the world's most endangered crane species.
''They are perhaps the least known of the cranes,'' Dr. Archibald said. 
''It was not until recent years that they were even photographed.''
The cranes nest in remote highlands on the Tibetan Plateau in China and migrate each winter to wetlands in southwestern China, Bhutan and northeastern India.
Several of the birds have been bred in zoos in China. 
Three years ago the Chinese Government donated a pair of the cranes to the Baraboo center, the first ever exported from China. 
A year later Vogelpark also received a pair.
The Baraboo cranes produced four eggs this year, but two broke. 
Aviculturists at the center used Florida sandhill cranes to incubate the eggs. 
One died, but after 31 days of incubation, the other egg hatched.
